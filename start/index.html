This points you at one route. It is a signpost, not a test — you can ignore it and browse.

Three questions

1. Have you worked in tech before?

If no, start at Help Desk / IT Support. It is the only role here that reliably hires people with no prior tech job.

2. Already doing help desk or support?

You have two realistic moves: Cloud Support if you like systems, or SOC Analyst if you like investigation.

3. How much can you spend?

Nothing, for a long time. Every route starts with free material, and we tell you the exact point where paying starts to be worth it — with the arithmetic shown.

Or just pick one

Accessibility Specialist

You make sure the product works for people the team forgot to think about.

Open without a degree, and one of the few specialisms where the entire governing standard is published free and you can learn it completely from the source. Legal pressure — public-sector requirements, the European Accessibility Act, litigation in the US — means organisations now hire for this rather than hoping. Disabled practitioners are especially valued here for lived expertise, and that is worth saying plainly.

AI Infrastructure Engineer

You run the clusters models train and serve on — GPUs, schedulers, networking and the things that fail at 3am.

Not an entry role. It assumes production infrastructure experience — the material below is Kubernetes and GPU serving, not an introduction to computing.

AI Platform Engineer

You build the internal platform other teams use to ship AI — so they do not each reinvent serving, tracking and deployment.

Not an entry role. It assumes you have operated cloud systems already — the material below starts at Kubernetes and platform design, not at fundamentals.

AI Product Engineer

You own whether an AI feature is actually good for the people using it — not just whether it runs.

Not an entry role. It assumes enough engineering to build the thing and enough product sense to evaluate it — usually reached from one side or the other rather than head-on.

AI Research Engineer

You build and run the experiments researchers design — training the models, not writing the papers.

Not an entry role. Of the three research-facing paths here it has the lowest barrier, because it is judged on engineering rather than publications — that is our reasoning from what the role assesses, not a hiring source we have checked. A PhD is common but not universal; demonstrated ability to reimplement papers is the substitute.

AI Research Scientist

You originate the ideas — formulate the problems, run the experiments, publish the results.

We are going to be blunt, because much of the internet is not. This role usually expects a PhD or an equivalent publication record, and no course or certificate substitutes for that. Its prerequisites are linear algebra, calculus and probability before any machine learning at all. If you are switching into tech and want a job this year, one of the other routes on this site is a better use of your time — this is a multi-year academic path, and saying otherwise would waste years of yours.

AI Safety Researcher

You work on making advanced AI systems behave as intended — interpretability, alignment, evaluation.

Research-level, with one real difference from the other two: this field runs structured entry courses precisely because it wants more people in it. That lowers the discovery barrier, not the difficulty. Technical alignment work still assumes strong ML; governance-facing work assumes policy literacy instead.

AI Security Engineer

You find and close the ways AI systems get attacked — prompt injection, data poisoning, model theft, unsafe tool use.

Not a first job. It assumes either security engineering or ML engineering already; the material below is standards and taxonomy, not an introduction to either field.

AI Software Engineer

You are a software engineer who builds features on top of AI models — the AI is a component, not the product.

Not a first tech job, but the shortest bridge into AI/ML for someone who already writes software — the prerequisites are Python and ML foundations, not a research background.

AI Solutions Architect

You design how AI systems fit together — and decide what not to build.

Not an entry role by any route. Architects are usually promoted from engineering after they have operated systems long enough to have opinions about failure.

Analytics Engineer

You turn raw tables into the trustworthy, documented ones everyone else queries.

A newer job title that sits between analysis and engineering, and one of the more reachable routes for someone who already writes good SQL. Open without a degree. The advantage over data engineering is that the skill floor is SQL rather than distributed systems; the advantage over analysis is that your work is version-controlled, tested and reused.

Application Security Engineer

You stop vulnerabilities from being written, rather than finding them after they ship.

Open without a degree, and one of the better-paid places a self-taught developer can end up. It is not an entry-level role: you need to read and write code well before the security layer means anything. The most common route is a few years of development followed by a deliberate turn toward security. Everything below is free.

Backend Developer

You build the part users never see — the APIs, the data layer, and everything that has to stay up.

A real no-degree route, though usually a slower one than frontend because progress is less visible early on. Hiring is portfolio-and-interview led: expect to be asked how you would handle failure, not just whether the happy path works.

Cloud Engineer

You design and run the infrastructure that applications live on, in code rather than by hand.

Not a first job. The honest route is help desk or cloud support first, then this. Direct entry happens with strong project evidence, not with a certificate alone.

Cloud Security Engineer

You make sure the cloud account nobody is watching is not the way in.

Open without a degree and in real demand, because most breaches now involve a misconfigured cloud resource rather than an exotic exploit. Not entry-level: you need to understand a cloud platform properly before securing it means anything. The usual route is cloud or devops work first, then a turn toward security. The vendor material below is free.

Cloud Support

You keep cloud infrastructure running and help engineers and customers when it misbehaves.

Good no-degree entry, most often reached via a help desk role first.

Data Analyst

You answer questions with data, and explain the answer to people who will not read your code.

A genuine no-degree route, and one of the more reachable ones, because SQL is testable in an interview and everything else is portfolio. Many people arrive from operations, finance or support roles where they already worked with spreadsheets.

Data Engineer

You build the pipes that get data from where it is made to where it gets used, reliably, every day.

Open without a degree, and a common step up from data analysis. Demand is steady because every organisation with analysts needs someone keeping their data arriving. The path is unusually concrete: get genuinely good at SQL, learn Python, then learn to schedule and monitor a pipeline.

Data Engineer (AI/ML)

You build and run the pipelines that get data to the models, reliably and on time.

A realistic way into AI/ML work for someone already in tech, because the skills transfer from ordinary backend and analytics work. We have not verified hiring volumes against a public labour-market source, so we are not going to claim this family's jobs are mostly here.

Data Scientist

You answer questions with data that nobody could answer by looking at a dashboard.

We have marked this one as NOT no-degree-friendly, and we want to be straight about why. Postings for this title very commonly ask for a quantitative degree, often a postgraduate one, and unlike most roles here that requirement is frequently real rather than decorative — the statistics are load-bearing. It is not impossible without one, and people do arrive from analysis, research or a scientific field. But if you are choosing a route into tech with no degree and no maths background, data analysis or data engineering are honest routes and this is a slower one. Our free-first position does not change; our claim about how open the door is does.

DevOps Engineer

You make shipping software boring — automated, repeatable, and safe to undo.

Rarely a first job, commonly a second or third. It assumes you have already operated or built something — the material below starts at containers and pipelines, not at fundamentals.

Distributed Systems Engineer

You build systems that stay correct when machines fail, networks partition and clocks disagree.

Not an entry role, and it does not pretend to be a short course. The canonical material is an MIT graduate subject; expect to work through papers and labs rather than tutorials.

Frontend Developer

You build the part of the software people actually touch — and make it work on their device, not just yours.

One of the genuinely open no-degree routes into software. Hiring is portfolio-led — what you have built and deployed matters more than what you have completed. Note the market is competitive at junior level, so deployed work matters more than finished courses.

Full-Stack Developer

You build the screen and the thing behind it, and you own the whole feature.

Very open without a degree — this is the classic self-taught route, and the free curriculum below runs from nothing to employable. The honest catch is that 'full-stack' at a junior level usually means being adequate at both halves, and you will be compared against specialists in interviews for either one. Deployed projects you can hand someone a link to are what close that gap.

GPU / CUDA Engineer

You make code run fast on GPUs — writing and tuning the kernels that everything else waits on.

Not an entry role. It assumes you already write C++ comfortably; CUDA is a specialisation on top. NVIDIA's own introductory tutorial assumes C++ familiarity and goes straight to kernels.

Help Desk / IT Support

You are the person who fixes it when someone's computer, login or printer stops working.

This is the canonical no-degree entry into tech. If you are starting from nothing, start here.

LLM Engineer

You build applications on top of large language models — retrieval, agents, evaluation and serving.

Not a first tech job. For someone who already ships software it is a shorter step than the research-facing roles, because much of the work is ordinary backend engineering applied to a model you did not train — that is our reasoning from the skill overlap, not a hiring-volume source we have verified.

Machine Learning Engineer

You take models from a notebook to something that runs reliably in production, and keep it working.

Honestly: this is not a no-degree cold-start role, and anyone telling you a single certificate opens it is selling something. The common entry routes we can point at are software, data and cloud engineering — that reflects the skill overlap and Google's own stated recommendation of 3+ years industry experience, not a survey of who gets hired.

MLOps Engineer

You make machine learning repeatable — training, tracking, deploying and watching models in production.

Not a first job. It is largely DevOps discipline applied to models, so the transfer from platform or infrastructure work is direct — that is our reasoning from the skill overlap, not a hiring source we have checked.

Mobile Developer

You build the app on the phone, where the network drops and the battery matters.

A real no-degree route, with the unusual advantage that both platform vendors publish complete free courses. A published app is the strongest thing you can bring, and publishing one is entirely within reach.

Network Administrator

You keep the network up — switches, routing, wifi, VPNs, and the mystery of why one floor can't print.

Rarely a first job, commonly a second one. The usual route is help desk first, then Network+ and internal moves.

Penetration Tester

You are paid to break in, with permission and in writing, and then explain exactly how.

Genuinely open without a degree — this field cares about demonstrable skill more than almost any other here. But it is rarely an entry-level job. In our reading of how these roles are advertised, people tend to arrive from help desk, networking, sysadmin or defensive security rather than straight off the street, and the free training below is excellent and deep.

Platform Engineer

You build the paved road other engineers ship on, so they stop reinventing the same pipeline.

Open without a degree, but not open from a standing start. Platform teams exist inside organisations big enough to have a shared internal toolchain, and they hire people who have already felt the pain of not having one. The usual route is devops or sysadmin work first. Learning Kubernetes deeply is free and is the single highest-leverage thing you can do here.

QA / Test Automation Engineer

You find out what breaks before a user does, and turn that into a test that runs forever.

One of the more open routes into software without a degree, partly because manual QA roles still exist and convert into automation from the inside. Being able to write a failing test that proves a bug is the skill that moves you up.

Security Analyst

You work out what an attacker did, or is doing, and write it up so someone can act.

Reachable without a degree, usually via IT support or networking rather than directly. Closely related to the SOC analyst route — that one is the shift-based entry point, this is the broader analysis role.

SOC Analyst

You watch the alerts, work out which ones matter, and investigate the ones that do.

Realistic without a degree, but usually reached via IT support or networking rather than as a first job. Direct entry happens with Security+ and genuine lab evidence.

Solutions Architect

You decide how the pieces fit together, and you have to defend the decision to people paying for it.

Open without a degree, but genuinely senior — nobody is hired into this from a course. It is where cloud, infrastructure and engineering careers arrive after several years, and the distinguishing skill is being able to justify a trade-off to a non-technical decision-maker. If you are early, this is a destination to aim at rather than a next step.

Site Reliability Engineer

You keep the thing running at scale, and you use software to do it rather than heroics.

Not an entry-level job, and we would be misleading you to suggest otherwise. It is genuinely open without a degree, but essentially everyone arrives from sysadmin, devops, backend or operations work. If you are starting from zero, start at help desk or devops and come back. The canonical texts below are free, and reading them early is not wasted.

Systems Administrator

You keep the servers, accounts and services running that everyone else quietly depends on.

Rarely a first job, commonly a second. The usual route is help desk first, then Linux depth and internal moves. Among the no-degree steps available once you are already in IT it has a low barrier — that is our reasoning from the prerequisites, not a hiring source we have checked.

Technical Writer

You make complicated things understandable, in writing, for someone in a hurry.

One of the most genuinely open routes here, and a common way into tech for people from teaching, journalism or support. Hiring is sample-led: two or three pieces of clear documentation are the whole application.

UX Designer

You decide how a product should work for the person using it, then prove the decision with evidence.

Genuinely no-degree friendly — hiring is portfolio-first in this field. What replaces a degree is three or four projects where you can explain the decisions, not the certificate you finished.

UX Researcher

You find out what people actually do, rather than what everyone assumes they do.

Open without a degree, though social-science backgrounds are common and do help. Portfolio-led like design: five well-run sessions written up honestly beat any qualification.
