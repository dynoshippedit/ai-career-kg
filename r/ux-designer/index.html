UX & Product Design

UX Designer

You decide how a product should work for the person using it, then prove the decision with evidence.

✓No degree needed ◆Moderate. The craft is learnable; the judgement takes longer.

Can I actually do this?

Genuinely no-degree friendly — hiring is portfolio-first in this field. What replaces a degree is three or four projects where you can explain the decisions, not the certificate you finished.

Who it suits. People who are curious about why someone got confused, and who can take criticism of work they made without taking it personally.

Runway. Months of deliberate practice building real interfaces, not years of study.

Coming from another job?

No prior tech role needed. People arrive from graphic design, teaching, retail and support work — anything that involved watching real people struggle with something.

The route

Four stations, in order. Each one is a thing you finish before the next matters.

  1. 1 Station one

    Learn it free

    Only the best few, deliberately. Every one of these is free to use — the pill on each card says exactly what is and isn't free.

    Usability 101 (Nielsen Norman Group)

    ✓Free to learn · no certificate

    The standard plain-English introduction to what usability actually means, from the research group the field defers to. Free to read; NN/g's courses are separately and expensively paid.

    Verified 2026-07-27

    ARIA Authoring Practices Guide (W3C)

    ✓Free to learn · no certificate

    How interface patterns should behave for assistive technology, from the body that writes the standard. Free and open. Accessibility is the part of this job most portfolios skip and most employers ask about.

    Verified 2026-07-27

    Atlassian Design System

    ✓Free to learn · no certificate

    A production design system published in full — foundations, components, and the reasoning behind them. Free to read, and closer to how design works inside a company than any tutorial.

    Verified 2026-07-27

  3. 3 Station three

    Prove it

    A certificate says you passed a test. These say you can do the job.

    A redesign with the reasoning shown

    Take something that frustrated you, redesign it, and document what you changed and why. The reasoning is the artifact — a pretty screenshot proves nothing.

    Five usability sessions

    Watch five people try to complete one task with something you made. Write down where they hesitated. This is the single most convincing thing a junior can bring to an interview.

    An accessibility pass

    Take one of your own designs and fix it against the ARIA patterns above — keyboard order, focus states, contrast. Document what was broken before.

    Who hires for this. Product companies, agencies, in-house teams at anything with a customer-facing app, and increasingly AI companies who need someone to make model output usable.

    Portfolios are read for reasoning, not polish. Being able to say why you rejected an option is what separates a hire from a folder of screenshots — that is our reading of how design hiring works, not a statistic we have verified.

    On salary

    We don't publish salary estimates. Numbers copied between blogs drift from reality, and a wrong number costs you real negotiating power. When we have a verified public source, it goes here with its date.
